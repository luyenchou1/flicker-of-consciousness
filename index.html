The Flicker of Consciousness

When your eyes receive two conflicting images, you consciously see only one. New recordings from the primate brain show that the neurons never settle: they alternate about 15 times per second between a pure copy of the image you see — and a pure copy of the one you don't.

An illustrated guide to Hesse, Lanfranchi, McGrory, Shi, Engel & Tsao, “Rapid alternation of conscious and suppressed stimulus representations in the primate brain” (UC Berkeley · Princeton · HHMI).

01The trick

Making an image invisible — while it's still there

To study consciousness, you need the physical world to stay constant while conscious experience changes. The team used binocular flash suppression: show one face to the left eye for a while, then suddenly flash a different face to the right eye. The new face takes over your awareness. The first face is still physically on screen, hitting your retina the entire time — you just stop seeing it.

02The 30-year puzzle

Neurons barely seemed to care what you see

Here's what has puzzled the field since the 1990s. Take a neuron that fires strongly for face A and weakly for face B when each is shown alone. During rivalry — when A is seen and B suppressed — that neuron should fire as if only A were present. It doesn't. Its selectivity collapses to roughly a quarter of normal, and the “invisible” face can still be decoded from the population.

The plots that carry this evidence — and the paper's punchline later on — all share one format, so here is the decoder ring. Every dot is one neuron, plotted by the answer to two questions: how much does it prefer face A over face B when each is shown alone (x-axis), and how much of that preference shows up during rivalry (y-axis)? 03The experiment

Watch hundreds of face cells, moment by moment

Older studies averaged across trials, blurring away anything fast. Here, the team inserted Neuropixels probes — recording hundreds of neurons simultaneously — into the face patches of two macaques: areas ML and AM in inferotemporal (IT) cortex, plus two face-selective regions of prefrontal cortex.

Face cells have a special property: each one responds linearly along a preferred axis in a mathematical “face space.” That makes the code readable — from population activity you can reconstruct the exact face being represented. By showing 1,500 different faces in flash suppression, the team could ask, for every 10-millisecond slice of a single trial: which face is the brain representing right now?

04The discovery

The brain flickers between two worlds

During a single trial — stimulus constant, percept stable — the population did not hold one representation. It snapped back and forth between two states: micro-epochs representing the conscious face and micro-epochs representing the suppressed face, each lasting about 60 ms.

This is far too fast to notice. Your experience feels like a steady image of face B — but underneath, the cortex is spending half its time representing a face you have no awareness of.

05Pure signals, at last

Each state carries one face — and only one

Using decoding weights trained on unambiguous trials, the team reconstructed the face represented in each state. The result is the paper's centerpiece: state 1 activity reconstructs the conscious face almost perfectly, with essentially zero trace of the suppressed one. State 2 is the mirror image — a high-fidelity picture of the face the animal cannot see.

06Is it real?

Ruling out the ways this could be an illusion

Sorting noisy data into two bins and then reading each bin back out can manufacture “states” from nothing — the authors say so themselves. So they stress-tested the finding. The decisive check: infer the states from one half of the neurons, reconstruct the faces from the other half. If the switching were just private noise in each cell, this would collapse. It didn't.

07Puzzle, solved — with a twist

Same neurons, two channels — and a tag for awareness

The alternation isn't two teams of neurons taking turns. The same cells encode both faces, switching which one they express (the gains correlate at r = 0.90 across cells). Split the data by state and the 30-year puzzle dissolves:

One asymmetry remains, and it may be the most important clue: tuning is ~1.21× steeper during conscious epochs, and a simple linear decoder can tell the two states apart (66% across all stimuli). The brain doesn't just carry both images — it marks which epochs are destined for awareness.

“Conscious access requires temporal selection beyond localization” — the signature of awareness isn't a place in the brain, but when, and with what amplification, a representation is expressed.
08Why it matters

Rewriting the search for consciousness

The same interleaved structure appeared in prefrontal cortex — so neither the “front” nor the “back” of the brain holds a pure conscious signal in its average activity. Both multiplex seen and unseen content in time. Teams hunting for the neural correlates of consciousness by comparing region-averaged activity (including recent adversarial collaborations) may have been averaging away the very signal they sought.

  • Unconscious influence, explained. Suppressed images are known to nudge behavior, attention, and emotion. Now we know why: the unseen image gets rich, full-fidelity cortical representation — just in epochs that never reach awareness.
  • A general coding trick? Rhythmic attention sampling, working-memory gamma bursts, orbitofrontal value flickering, hippocampal map flickering — time-sharing may be how the cortex routinely keeps multiple representations alive in one population.
  • The next question. If both images get their moments, what routes only some micro-epochs into awareness, report, and behavior? The decodable “conscious tag” suggests a dynamic gating mechanism — perhaps the suppressed epochs are the brain privately exploring alternative interpretations of the world.
Read with care: this is a preprint (posted Oct 16, 2026), not yet peer-reviewed. Data come from two macaques viewing faces in flash suppression; whether the same temporal multiplexing holds for other stimuli, other rivalry dynamics, and human brains is open. Coordination of the flicker across brain areas was weak — each region may run on its own clock.
09Philosophical implications

Does this vindicate a functionalist view of consciousness?

Partly — and it helps to separate two claims. The weaker one is physicalism: that conscious experience traces to physical, neurological events. These results are striking evidence for tight neural correlates — you can reconstruct what the animal is seeing from the right 60-millisecond slices of activity. But correlates alone don't settle the metaphysics; even a dualist can accept that experience is accompanied by brain activity. And note what's being measured: conscious access — which contents become available for report and behavior (what Ned Block calls access consciousness) — not phenomenal experience itself.

The stronger, more interesting boost goes to functionalism — the view that what makes a state conscious is the causal role it plays, not where it sits or what content it carries. The key observation: the seen and unseen faces get identical representational content. Same neurons, same feature code, comparable fidelity. So content can't be what makes an epoch conscious. At the same time, the result embarrasses the localizationist framing of nearly every theory. The field's flagship debate — is consciousness in the front of the brain or the back? — presupposed that somewhere, average activity would cleanly reflect experience. Here, neither prefrontal nor sensory cortex holds a pure conscious signal in its average; the distinction lives in time, gain, and routing, not in place.

The honest summary: strong evidence that conscious access is a specific, mechanistically tractable physical process; real support for role-based (functionalist) accounts over content- or location-based ones; and deliberate silence on the hard problem — why the amplified, tagged epochs are accompanied by experience at all.

This section is interpretation beyond the preprint: the authors frame their finding as “temporal selection beyond localization” and stop short of adjudicating between philosophical theories of consciousness.
